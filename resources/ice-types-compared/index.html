Ice Types Compared: Flake, Nugget, Cube, Crescent, Bullet

Six ice shapes, six different jobs. Choosing the wrong one is the most common specification mistake we see — and it cannot be fixed by buying a bigger machine.

The comparison

Melt speed is the variable most buyers underestimate. Fast-melting ice chills a product quickly but disappears from a glass; slow-melting ice looks better in service but does less cooling work per kilogram.

How to choose in practice

Start with what the ice touches. If it touches the product directly — fish, fillets, shellfish — you need flake, because flake moulds around the surface and chills by contact. If it touches a glass, you need cube, crescent or nugget, because the customer sees it and it has to look right.

Then decide how fast it disappears. Nugget and flake melt faster than cube. In a display bed that means more top-ups per day; in a drink it means faster dilution. Neither is a defect — it is the trade-off the shape was designed for.

Finally check the duty cycle. A shape that works at home does not automatically work in a bar. Bullet ice from a countertop machine is fine for a household fridge; a working bar well needs a machine rated to recover during service.

Common mis-specifications

  • Cube ice for a fish counter. Cube leaves gaps, chills slowly and bruises the product. Use flake or nugget.
  • Nugget ice for a bar well. It dilutes fast and disappears from the bin during peak service. Use cube or crescent.
  • Flake ice for drinks. It melts almost immediately and turns the drink watery. Flake is a working ice, not a serving ice.
  • Buying for the bin, not the recovery. A 100 kg bin does not help if the machine can only add 4 kg per hour during service.
If you are unsure, describe the end use — what the ice touches, how long it must last and how many servings per hour. That answers the shape question faster than comparing specifications.
