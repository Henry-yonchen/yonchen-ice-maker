Three worked examples

A 60-cover restaurant, two services. 60 × 0.75 kg × 2 = 90 kg. Add 20% for a normal busy day: 108 kg/24h. A 100–120 kg machine with a bin of at least 15 kg covers it.

A 150-cover restaurant with a terrace. 150 × 0.8 kg × 2 = 240 kg, plus 20% = 288 kg/24h. The terrace is why we do not size at 240 — outdoor seating extends the service and raises per-cover consumption.

A 90-seat bar, cocktails. 90 × 1.0 kg = 90 kg per night, plus 20% = 108 kg/24h. But a bar draws ice in bursts: the bin needs to cover the 9pm–11pm window, not the whole night.

Where the arithmetic breaks down

Three things push real consumption above the planning figure, and all three are common:

  • Ice as a display. A seafood or buffet display consumes ice continuously to replace melt, and it is rarely counted in per-cover maths.
  • Staff and spillage. A scoop held too long, a bin left open, a machine that is switched off overnight and has to rebuild.
  • Summer. Both consumption and machine output move in the wrong direction: guests drink more, the machine makes less.
These are planning figures, not guarantees. If you have a week of measured consumption — bags in, bags out — use that number instead. Measured data beats any table, including this one.

FAQ

Questions

How much ice per customer?

For a full-service restaurant, plan 0.5–1.0 kg per cover per service. Beverage-led venues sit at the top of that range; food-led venues with a small drinks menu sit at the bottom. A bar serving cocktails is higher again, around 0.8–1.2 kg per cover.

Should I size for the busiest day or the average day?

Neither, exactly. Size for a normal busy day plus 20%, then check the machine can still keep up on your peak day with the bin acting as a buffer. Sizing for the single busiest day of the year means an oversized machine running at part load for the other 364.

Does the bin matter as much as the output?

They answer different questions. Daily output answers whether the machine can keep up across the day; bin size answers whether it survives a rush. A venue with a short, intense peak needs a larger bin even when the daily total already looks sufficient.
