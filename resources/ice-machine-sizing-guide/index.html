How to Size an Ice Machine

A method that starts from what you actually consume per day, adds headroom for peaks and ambient heat, and lands on a model — instead of guessing from the room size.

5Steps
+20%Headroom
−15%Hot ambient
76Models to fit

The five steps

1. Count consumption, not space. Work out how much ice disappears on a normal day — in drinks, on display, in processing. Room size tells you nothing about ice demand.

2. Add the peak factor. A normal day is not your design day. Add 20% for weekend and seasonal peaks, more for a venue with a terrace or a summer season.

3. Correct for ambient temperature. Rated output is measured under standard conditions. Above roughly 35 °C in the machine room, expect 15–20% less real output from the same model. Size up rather than run the machine flat out.

4. Check the recovery rate. Daily output tells you the total; recovery tells you whether the bin refills during service. If the bin empties at 7 p.m. and refills at midnight, the daily number was fine and the machine was still the wrong choice.

5. Pick the smallest model that clears the number. Oversizing costs money up front and runs the machine at part load, which is not more efficient in practice. Undersizing is worse — it means continuous duty and early wear.

Planning estimates by venue

These are widely used starting points for a first calculation. They are estimates, not specifications — a week of measured consumption beats any table.

Restaurant (full service)0.5–1.0 kg per cover per serviceCovers × kg × services
Café / coffee shop0.2–0.3 kg per iced drinkIced drinks per day × kg
Bar / cocktail0.8–1.2 kg per cover per nightCovers × kg
Hotel banquet0.5 kg per cover + display iceCovers × kg + buffet ice
Supermarket display1.5–2× bed volume per shiftBed volume × factor × shifts
Seafood processingFollows throughput, not coverskg of product per day × ratio

Worked example: 120-cover restaurant

Two services a day, 120 covers each, mostly à la carte with a bar menu. Using 0.7 kg per cover: 120 × 0.7 = 84 kg per service, × 2 services = 168 kg per day. That is the consumption figure before headroom.

Add 20% for weekend peaks: 168 × 1.2 ≈ 202 kg/24h. If the machine sits in a hot kitchen above 35 °C, add a further 15%: ≈ 232 kg/24h. The specification is therefore a machine in the 230–250 kg/24h band, not a 200 kg unit.

Two 120 kg units would also clear the number, and give redundancy — if one is serviced the kitchen keeps trading. For a single-site restaurant with no backup plan, that is often the better commercial decision.
